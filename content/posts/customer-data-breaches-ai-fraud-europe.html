How stolen customer data fuels fraud, where AI changes the threat, and why Europe needs stronger enforcement.

TL;DR

  • Notifications are rising: official figures show increases in France and the Netherlands, although notifications are not the same as separate attacks or individual victims.
  • AI accelerates abuse: it helps criminals personalise scams, process stolen information and produce convincing impersonations. There is no reliable official measurement establishing what percentage of breaches AI caused.
  • Basic protection still fails: regulators continue to uncover weak authentication, shared staff accounts and customer passwords stored in plain text.
  • Contact details have lasting value: even when passwords and payment information are unaffected, stolen data gives criminals material for believable messages, calls and identity fraud.
  • Protection requires action on both sides: Europe needs faster investigations and verified remediation. Customers should secure their email, use unique passwords or passkeys, verify unexpected requests and revoke suspicious account permissions.

Two emails in a week

This week, I received an email from SNIPES telling me that customer data had been compromised. A week earlier, another merchant had sent a similar notification.

Receiving two notices so close together creates an uncomfortable question: how many businesses hold information about me that they cannot adequately protect?

A breach notification also marks the beginning of a different problem. The merchant can investigate, restore systems and close a vulnerability. Customers may have to live with the exposed information for years.

To understand whether these incidents are multiplying, we need to distinguish three things: how often breaches are reported, how effectively organisations protect data, and what criminals do after obtaining it.

Research checked: 25 November 2026. The opening describes my experience of receiving the emails. The SNIPES notice has not been independently authenticated for this article, and its scope, cause and any subsequent misuse are not established here. The investigation below concerns the wider evidence.

What the figures actually show

France provides a useful official series:

  • 2023: 4,668 personal-data breach notifications.
  • 2024: 5,629 notifications, approximately 20% above 2023.
  • 2025, adjusted headline figure: 6,167 notifications. The CNIL reports a 9.5% increase.
  • 2025, before that adjustment: 17,802 notifications, including 11,635 arising from two supplier incidents.

Sources: the CNIL’s 2023 cybersecurity publication, 2024 annual review and 2025 annual report, printed page 52.

The adjustment matters. Two compromised software providers generated thousands of notifications from their business customers. Reporting the raw increase as thousands of separate successful attacks would exaggerate what the figures establish. The 2025 report also gives its 2024 comparator as 5,630, one above the previously published figure.

The Netherlands recorded 39,407 notifications in 2025, compared with 37,839 in 2024—an increase of approximately 4.1%, calculated from the regulator’s totals. Its authority explicitly warns that phishing can be both a cause and a consequence of breaches. Dutch data-protection authority, July 2026.

These figures support concern, but they do not constitute a global census of hacking. A personal-data breach can also involve an email sent to the wrong recipient, lost equipment or accidental disclosure. National totals should not be added together as though reporting practices and populations were identical.

Nor does every indicator rise. CERT-EU handled nine significant incidents in 2025, down from 15 in 2024, within its specific remit covering EU institutions and associated bodies. CERT-EU’s 2025 review.

Read the denominator

Several official reporting systems show increasing exposure. The evidence does not establish one universal growth rate for customer-data theft.

AI makes stolen data easier to exploit

AI affects both sides of a breach: gaining access to information and exploiting it afterwards.

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre identifies existing malicious uses involving reconnaissance, social engineering, basic malware generation and processing stolen data. It assesses that AI will make elements of intrusion more effective and increase the threat, while distinguishing observed capabilities from forecasts through 2027. NCSC threat assessment.

For customers, the immediate concern is the falling cost of persuasion. Criminals can generate fluent messages, translate them into different languages and maintain convincing conversations with many targets. The European Commission’s summary of Europol’s 2026 assessment describes generative AI as an accelerator of personalised social engineering and online fraud. European Commission, April 2026.

A hypothetical example

A stolen customer record contains a name, telephone number and recent support interaction. An attacker uses those details to construct a plausible follow-up message about a refund. AI can help produce and adapt the conversation; the stolen information supplies credibility.

Voice cloning adds another layer. The FBI has warned about AI-generated audio impersonating relatives and other trusted people, alongside fabricated identification documents and synthetic video. However, an ordinary contact-data breach does not itself provide the voice recording needed for cloning. That material would have to come from elsewhere. FBI warning on AI-enabled fraud.

There is an important limit to the evidence: these assessments do not establish what proportion of merchant breaches AI caused. AI is demonstrably useful to attackers, but blaming every breach on AI can obscure older failures.

Businesses are still failing at basic protection

Retail incidents are well documented. In May 2025, the UK Information Commissioner confirmed receiving reports from Marks & Spencer and Co-op and said it was making enquiries. That established regulatory scrutiny, rather than a finding of negligence. ICO statement.

A breach alone cannot establish that a merchant spent too little on security. That requires evidence about its controls, decisions and foreseeable risks.

Regulatory decisions nevertheless show that inadequate protection remains a concrete problem.

On 18 November 2026, the CNIL announced a €5 million fine against an unnamed company. Inspectors found customer passwords stored in plain text, shared employee credentials for accessing customer databases, and excessive data retention. The authority said some improvements occurred during the enforcement process. CNIL decision announcement.

In January 2026, the CNIL announced €42 million in combined fines against Free Mobile and Free following an intrusion involving data associated with 24 million subscriber contracts. It found insufficiently robust VPN authentication and ineffective detection of abnormal behaviour. These are contracts, not necessarily 24 million distinct people. CNIL’s Free decisions.

These examples extend beyond retail, but they demonstrate a broader failure: organisations holding large customer databases still lack elementary safeguards.

The standard customers should expect includes restricted access, individual staff accounts, secure authentication, timely patching, effective monitoring and deletion when information is no longer needed. Suppliers deserve the same scrutiny. A business’s security boundary includes the organisations through which customer information is accessible.

What happens to stolen customer data

The consequences depend on what was exposed. An email address, a reusable password and an identity-document scan create different risks.

  • Name, email address or telephone number: targeted phishing and impersonation.
  • Customer relationship or purchase details: believable delivery, refund or support pretexts.
  • Reused login credentials: attempts to access accounts at other services.
  • Address, date of birth or identity documents: identity fraud and attempts to defeat verification.
  • Banking information: more convincing bank impersonation and potential payment fraud.

French official guidance identifies phishing, account compromise and identity theft as consequences of leaked personal information. The CNIL specifically warns that fraudsters can use knowledge of an IBAN to impersonate a bank adviser and pressure someone into authorising a payment. Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr; CNIL guidance on exposed banking details.

Credential stuffing is particularly direct: attackers take stolen username-and-password pairs and try them against other services. Password reuse makes one company’s breach a possible entry point into another account. CNIL explanation.

This is why “no payment details were affected” describes only part of the risk. Contact information can help an attacker persuade the victim to provide the missing credentials or approve a fraudulent transaction.

Recent attacks show why changing a password may be insufficient

Several documented techniques illustrate how the threat is evolving. They are not evidence that data from the SNIPES incident has been used in these ways.

Device-code phishing

In May 2026, the FBI warned about Kali365, a subscription phishing platform offering AI-generated lures. Victims are instructed to enter a supplied code on a genuine Microsoft verification page. Doing so can authorise an attacker-controlled device and give the attacker account tokens. A legitimate website can therefore be part of a malicious request. FBI alert, 21 May 2026.

Malicious application permissions

A September 2026 FBI warning described messages directing targets to legitimate permission screens, where they unknowingly authorise a malicious application. The resulting access can include reading email and files. The FBI stresses that changing a password does not remove this permission: the malicious application’s access must be revoked. FBI alert, 1 September 2026.

Voice phishing against business systems

CERT-EU’s 2025 review describes a campaign in which attackers used calls to persuade employees at more than 90 organisations worldwide to authorise malicious applications in Salesforce environments. It also documents compromised partner email accounts being used to approach further targets. CERT-EU review.

Identity fraud using synthetic documents

US Treasury agency FinCEN has documented criminals combining AI-generated images with stolen personal information to create fraudulent identities. Its analysis includes accounts successfully opened using fraudulent identities suspected of involving generative AI. This provides an explicit connection between stolen information and AI-assisted financial crime, although it does not mean a contact-data leak alone is sufficient to open an account. FinCEN alert.

The common thread is the abuse of trust. Criminals can exploit what they know about a person, impersonate an existing relationship, or manipulate a legitimate authorisation process.

Europe needs enforcement that produces measurable protection

Europe already has breach-reporting obligations. Under the GDPR, controllers generally must notify the relevant authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of a breach unless it is unlikely to pose a risk to individuals. Where a breach is likely to create a high risk, affected people must also be informed without undue delay, subject to the applicable exceptions. The 72-hour deadline is not a universal deadline for emailing customers. European Data Protection Board guidance.

Enforcement is happening. In 2025, the CNIL imposed 83 sanctions totalling approximately €487 million. It also announced that half its inspections and enforcement actions in 2026 would focus on data security. These are broader regulatory figures, not a tally of breach penalties alone. CNIL’s 2025 review.

Cross-border procedures are also changing. Regulation (EU) 2025/2518 introduces harmonised procedural rules and investigation deadlines. The Council describes a general 15-month investigation period, extendable by 12 months for complex cases. The regulation applies from 2 April 2027, so it should not be presented as already operational in November 2026. Council explanation; European Commission implementation summary.

My assessment is that enforcement should now be judged against four practical outcomes:

  • Faster correction: serious weaknesses should be addressed promptly, with verification that fixes work.
  • Supplier accountability: investigations should examine how access was granted, supervised and withdrawn across providers.
  • Useful notifications: customers need clear information about affected data, remaining uncertainty and appropriate action.
  • Comparable statistics: public reporting should separate notifications, underlying incidents, malicious attacks and affected individuals.

Fine totals measure regulatory activity. They do not, by themselves, show whether customer information has become safer.

How to protect yourself

1. Verify the notice independently

Open the merchant’s official website or app yourself. Use independently obtained contact details when checking a breach notification or an unexpected follow-up. Preserve the original notice and ask which categories of your information were affected. CNIL guidance for affected individuals.

2. Secure your email and eliminate password reuse

Change exposed passwords and any reused elsewhere. Use a password manager to maintain unique passwords. Enable passkeys where available, or strong passwords with two-step verification. Keep recovery methods secure too. The NCSC recommends passkeys for important accounts because they resist common credential-phishing attacks. NCSC account-security guidance.

3. Review permissions after a suspicious request

If you entered an unsolicited device code or approved an unfamiliar application, inspect account security settings and revoke suspicious access. In consent-phishing cases, a password change alone may leave the attacker connected. FBI remediation guidance.

4. Treat accurate personal details as insufficient proof of identity

Someone knowing your name, address, merchant relationship or banking details may be using stolen information. End an unexpected financial call and contact the organisation through a trusted channel before taking action. CNIL warning on bank impersonation.

5. Monitor for misuse and act on evidence

Watch for unfamiliar transactions, invoices and accounts. If money or identity information is misused, contact the relevant financial institution promptly, preserve messages and other evidence, and report the fraud to police. Identity-theft procedures vary by country; Germany’s European Consumer Centre provides a useful local recovery checklist. European Consumer Centre Germany.

6. Reduce unnecessary future exposure

Close unused accounts and request deletion of information that no longer needs to be retained. Legal retention requirements may limit deletion, and deleting a merchant account cannot retrieve copies already stolen. French government guidance explains when individuals can request erasure. Service-Public guidance.

Customers can make fraud harder. They cannot repair a merchant’s authentication, audit its suppliers or control how long it keeps their information. Those responsibilities belong to the organisations collecting the data—and to the authorities enforcing the rules.
