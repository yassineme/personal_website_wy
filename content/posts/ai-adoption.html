A successful demonstration answers one question: can the system do something useful? Adoption asks a wider one: can people use it confidently as part of the real work?

Those questions need different evidence. A technical test can show that an assistant retrieves relevant information. It cannot, on its own, show whether people understand when to use it, whether they can challenge an answer, or whether reviewing the output is worth their time.

My starting point for an adoption conversation is to examine the distance between the demonstration and an ordinary working day.

Look at the whole task.

Map what happens immediately before and after the moment where AI is intended to help. Where does the input come from? What does the person do with the output? Who receives it next?

A suggestion that appears in a separate application may require someone to copy information, check permissions, repeat an explanation or reconcile two versions. Each step belongs in the assessment of the proposed improvement.

A useful observation

Ask someone to complete a real task. Watch the handoffs, checks and workarounds that the demonstration did not include.

Make the system understandable.

Give people a clear account of what the tool is intended to support, where its limits are and how to recognise a situation that needs another route. Explain the benefit in the language of their work.

Google PAIR’s guidance on mental models connects expectations with how people understand and use an AI product. It recommends staged introductions and explanations grounded in the user’s task.1

An onboarding conversation can be simple: here is the task this supports; here is what to check; here is when to stop; here is who can help. The details should reflect the actual system and the consequences of a mistake.

Give feedback somewhere to go.

“Tell us what you think” is an invitation. An operating feedback loop also needs a recipient, a review rhythm and a way to explain what changed.

Let users report an unhelpful answer or an awkward step close to where it happens. Distinguish an output-quality problem from a missing feature, an outdated source or a process disagreement. Different problems need different owners.

A small community of users can help surface recurring friction, provided its purpose is to improve the work and its feedback can influence delivery priorities.

Make support part of the rollout.

Before expanding access, establish who responds when someone is blocked, who owns the underlying information and who can authorise a change to the workflow.

Bring process owners, delivery teams and users into that discussion early. If the solution changes a handoff between teams, both sides need to understand the new arrangement.

This is where leadership becomes practical: give people a route to resolve ambiguity and enough time to learn the changed task.

Measure usefulness after the first try.

Initial sign-ups and first-use activity show that people have encountered the tool. Add evidence about whether it helps them complete the intended task.

Look at repeat use in context, the quality of completed work, time spent checking or correcting outputs, and the problems that cause people to return to the old process. Pair those signals with conversations; a usage count rarely explains the reason behind a behaviour.

Illustrative example

A drafting assistant

A team may generate many draft reports in the first week. To understand usefulness, also examine how much of each draft survives review, which sections require correction, and whether the complete reporting task becomes easier. More generated text alone does not answer those questions.

Use a short adoption review.

Bring delivery and operations together around these questions:

  • Which real task is now easier, and for whom?
  • Where are people still creating workarounds?
  • Do users know what to check and when to ask for help?
  • Which feedback has changed the product or process?
  • Who owns the next improvement?

Adoption becomes easier to discuss when it is tied to observable work. The next action might be a product change, clearer guidance, a process decision or a smaller rollout. Each is more useful than treating resistance as a single problem with a single answer.
