Notes on change / Operating models

From AI experiments to everyday value.

The recurring decisions and responsibilities that connect a promising experiment to something an organisation can sustain.

Yassine W. MedjatiUpdated 25 Oct 20263 min read

In this note

What you can use

Take a five-point leadership agenda into the discussion about what should scale.

An operating model makes the recurring decisions around AI explicit: what to pursue, who can approve it, how the work is maintained and how people get support when something goes wrong.

A portfolio of pilots creates an opportunity to learn. The next step is to decide which practices should become repeatable, which responsibilities need to be formalised and which ideas should remain bounded experiments.

Start with a common decision frame.

Review use cases against the business problem, delivery readiness and the conditions for adoption. Make dependencies visible so that one team’s promising idea does not become another team’s unexplained commitment.

For each initiative, describe the next uncertainty and the evidence needed to resolve it. This gives leadership a way to compare the work without pretending that every idea is at the same stage.

The companion note on AI roadmap prioritisation develops a five-decision frame for that conversation.

Make choices around the workflow.

Assess technology options against the work they must support: the information involved, integration needs, reliability, human review and the effort required to run the solution.

Ask where a person needs to review, decide or intervene, and what happens when the system cannot complete the task. Define a fallback that people can actually use.

The appropriate level of autonomy depends on the task and the consequences of an error. A clear description of the workflow helps the team make that decision together.

Name the responsibilities that continue.

Delivery teams need a clear route to build and test. Operating teams need to know who maintains the solution, monitors its usefulness, supports users and decides whether it should change.

Capture those responsibilities before the initial project ends. Include responsibility for the underlying information and for changes to the process around the tool.

Bring this question into the room

If the pilot succeeds tomorrow, who will own it six months from now?

Create a review rhythm.

A repeatable review can bring together usefulness, delivery progress, user feedback and emerging concerns. Keep the review focused on decisions: continue, revise, expand, pause or retire.

NIST’s voluntary AI Risk Management Framework is a useful related resource for integrating trustworthiness considerations across an AI system’s lifecycle.1 A team still needs to translate that broader guidance into named responsibilities and task-specific checks.

The aim is an approach that can learn from use while keeping ownership clear. That is the connection between an experiment and something an organisation can sustain.

A starting agenda for leadership.

  1. Agree which operating problem deserves attention first.
  2. Identify the evidence required before the next investment decision.
  3. Describe human review and the fallback route.
  4. Name the delivery, process and support owners.
  5. Set a date to review usefulness, feedback and the next decision.

Use this as a conversation starter. The answers should come from the people who understand the business problem, the technology and the day-to-day work.
